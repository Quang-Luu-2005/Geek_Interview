\section{Expected Workload and Evaluation Goal}
The stated peak of 300--500 booking requests per minute is approximately
5--8.3 requests per second. The evaluation therefore targets stable behavior
at that rate and a short burst above it. It does not claim a universal capacity
limit: flash-sale risk is dominated by hot-row contention, transaction length,
and controlled conflict behavior rather than aggregate request volume alone.

\section{Executed Scenarios}
The k6 script ran 5 iterations/second for 30 seconds, followed by a three-stage
burst that ramped to 15 iterations/second and back over 30 seconds. It generated
unique idempotency keys so each iteration represented a new logical booking.
The rate limit was raised only to prevent the resilience guard from obscuring
booking/database behavior.

Hot inventory, final-voucher, sold-out, and lifecycle contention are covered by
dedicated deterministic concurrency suites. They were not represented as
separate latency/capacity scenarios in this k6 baseline; presenting them as
measured load results would overstate the evidence.

\section{Interpretation and Limitations}
The measured baseline is comfortably inside the stated workload and configured
thresholds on one local API/database pair. It demonstrates absence of 5xx or
unexpected status under this profile, not production capacity, multi-instance
behavior, or saturation. CPU allocation to Docker, database locality, connection
pool limits, and the non-hot STANDARD category materially influence the result.

Before a production claim, execute longer steady-state and soak tests; isolate
sold-out and single-hot-category latency; measure final-voucher contention;
capture database connection/lock wait/CPU/I/O; and repeat with the intended
deployment topology and shared rate limiter. Optimization should preserve short
transactions and deterministic resource order; admission control, partitioning,
or queueing needs measured hot-row evidence.
